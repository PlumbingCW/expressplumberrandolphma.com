What a tankless unit needs that a tank does not

A tank heater stores 40 or 50 gallons and reheats it. A tankless unit fires a large burner or element only when you open a tap, which is why it needs a bigger gas line or a heavier electrical circuit than a tank of the same household size.

Venting is the other difference. Condensing gas units run cool enough to use plastic vent pipe, and they produce acidic condensate that must drain to a proper receptor with a neutralizer. A cold basement corner can freeze a condensate line, so placement matters in a New England house.

Inlet screen and filter

A small mesh screen at the cold inlet catches grit. A clogged screen cuts flow and triggers a low-flow fault.

Heat exchanger

Scale builds up inside the heat exchanger over time. A flush with a descaling solution restores heat transfer.

Annual tankless flush: how it works

Isolate the unit

Close the supply valves and open the service valves on the unit.

Circulate descaler

A pump circulates a mild acid solution through the heat exchanger for about 45 minutes.

Rinse and clean the screen

Clean water flushes out the descaler, and the inlet screen is pulled and cleaned.

Test and record

We check temperature rise, flow and error codes and write the readings down.

Randolph's water is soft, at 39.6 ppm in the 2024 report, so scale builds more slowly here than in a hard-water town. Many units can go longer between flushes, and we check the exchanger rather than flushing on a calendar.

Tankless or tank: a fair comparison

Where tankless wins

  • Endless hot water for long showers.
  • Smaller wall footprint.
  • No standby heat loss from a stored tank.
  • Long service life when maintained.

Where a tank wins

  • Lower installation cost.
  • Simple gas and venting requirements.
  • Works during short outages if the pilot is on.
  • Easier for any plumber to service.

Condensing tankless units in New England

Condensing models reach higher efficiency by capturing heat from the exhaust, which turns into condensate. The condensate line must slope to a drain and stay above freezing. If it clogs or freezes, the unit shuts down with a fault code.

A frozen vent or a blocked intake in a snowstorm is one of the winter calls we see on tankless units. If your unit stops during a cold snap and shows a vent code, check outside for ice or snow around the vent termination, and call the 24/7 line if the unit does not restart.
