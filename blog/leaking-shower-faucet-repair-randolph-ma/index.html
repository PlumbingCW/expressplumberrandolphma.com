Leaking Shower Faucet Repair in Randolph, MA: What to Do

By the %s team. Published November 13, 2026. Updated November 13, 2026.

A shower faucet that drips after you turn it off is wasting water every minute of every day. A single drip per second adds up to thousands of gallons a year. The good news is that most shower leaks come from one worn part inside the valve.

This guide walks through the causes, the type of valve you have, a basic repair and the signs that the leak is inside the wall.

Why a shower faucet drips

A shower valve is a mixing valve. It blends hot and cold water and sends the mix to the showerhead or the tub spout. Inside, a cartridge, a set of washers or a ceramic disc seals the water when the handle is off. When that seal wears out, water slips past it and drips.

The leak can show at the showerhead, at the tub spout or around the handle. Each location points to a different part. A drip from the showerhead means the valve is not closing fully. A drip from the handle means a worn O-ring or packing. A drip from the tub spout when the shower is on means the diverter has failed.

Hard water or soft water

Randolph's water is soft, at 39.6 ppm in the 2024 report, so scale is less of an issue than in many towns. Cartridges here wear out from use and age.

Pressure and wear

High pressure speeds wear. A pressure regulator that has failed can shorten the life of every valve in the house.

Basic cartridge replacement

This applies to a single-handle valve. Check your brand and model before ordering a cartridge.

Shut off the water

Close the main valve or the fixture shutoff if one exists.

Remove the handle

Pop off the cap, remove the screw and pull the handle and trim plate.

Pull the retaining clip

The cartridge sits behind a clip or nut. Remove it with pliers.

Extract the cartridge

Grip the stem and pull straight out, noting its orientation.

Install the new cartridge

Push the new one in the same way, replace the clip and reassemble.

Test

Turn the water on slowly and check for drips at the spout and showerhead.

If the old cartridge will not come out, stop. A stuck cartridge can crack the valve body, and that turns a small repair into a wall opening.

Signs the leak is inside the wall

A leak behind the tile is a different problem from a worn cartridge.

  • A stain or bulge on the wall or ceiling below. Water is traveling along the pipe.
  • Loose or soft tile near the valve. Water has weakened the backer board.
  • A musty smell in the bathroom. Moisture is trapped in the wall.
  • A water bill that rose with no change in habits. A leak that does not show can still run the meter.

Our leak detection service uses moisture meters and thermal tools to find a wall leak before tile is removed.

Randolph bathrooms and their valves

The Massachusetts Historical Commission's survey of Randolph describes houses from the colonial period to the postwar suburbs. Bathrooms in houses from the 1950s and 1960s often have two-handle valves with compression stems, and many have been patched for decades. Those stems and seats wear out and can be replaced without opening the wall in many cases.

A replacement valve is a different job. The state's fact sheet says replacing or relocating a faucet, valve or piping is not a minor repair, and a licensed plumber pulls the permit.

What a small drip costs over a year

A shower that drips once a second loses about five gallons a day, which is more than 1,800 gallons a year. If the drip is hot water, you also pay to heat it. A leaking cartridge is one of the cheapest repairs in the house, and the water saved often covers the part within a few months.

The other cost is to the wall. A valve that leaks inside the wall keeps the backer board damp and can soften grout, loosen tile and feed mold. By the time a stain shows on the ceiling below, the repair involves more than a cartridge. Fixing a drip early is the simple way to avoid that.

If you are not sure whether a drip is in the valve or in the wall, close the shower supply at the fixture stop, if there is one, or at the main valve overnight. A bathroom that stays dry means the leak was at the valve. A wall that stays damp points to a pipe.
