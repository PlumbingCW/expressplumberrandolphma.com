What an emergency plumber in Randolph deals with first

A real emergency plumber call has a short list of jobs, and they are the same on most nights. Water that will not stop, sewage coming up through a floor drain, a water heater leaking into a finished basement, and a boiler that quit in January are the calls that cannot wait until morning.

When you phone the 24/7 line, the first questions are practical. Where is the water coming from, can you reach the main shutoff, and is anything electrical wet? Those answers decide whether the first visit is a burst pipe repair, a drain cleaning visit or a water heater repair.

Shut the water off before you dial

Find the main shutoff where the line enters the house, usually in the basement near the meter. Turn it fully clockwise. If it will not move, say so on the call, because that changes what the plumber brings.

Keep people and power away from the wet area

Water and live circuits do not mix. If water is near outlets, a panel or a sump pump cord, stay out of the room and tell the dispatcher before anything else.

Randolph homes and the plumbing behind the walls

Randolph grew in layers. A state survey of the town's buildings describes farmhouses and Federal houses near Randolph Center and along Main Street, side-hall cottages from the 1800s, and then a long run of suburban houses as the population climbed from 9,982 in 1950 to 29,206 in 1975. Capes, bungalows and ranches fill most of that postwar growth.

That mix matters in a plumbing call. An old center-chimney house can still have supply lines, drains and shutoffs that were patched over decades, while a 1960s ranch more often has copper supply lines and a cast iron or clay line to the street. Knowing the era tells a plumber where leaks tend to start, so the first visit goes straight to the likely spot instead of opening walls at random.

If your home sits in either group, a leak detection visit or a sewer camera inspection is usually cheaper than guessing. The camera shows root intrusion or a sagging section in minutes, and electronic tracing narrows a hidden leak to a patch of wall or floor.

Soft water, old pipes and what that means for you

Randolph and Holbrook share a water system fed by the Great Pond reservoirs. The town's 2024 drinking water report lists hardness of 39.6 ppm, which is soft. Scale builds up slowly in water heaters and showerheads here, so a failing tank is more often about age and corrosion than minerals.

The same report asked residents to flush taps for several minutes when water has sat unused, because one of 30 sampled homes tested above the lead action level. If you are replacing old fixtures or piping anyway, ask the plumber about repiping or new shutoff valves in the same visit.
